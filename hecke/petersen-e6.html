The Petersen Graph is a Shadow of E₆

How the most famous graph in combinatorics hides inside the geometry of cubic surfaces, and why this is not a coincidence.

Two famous objects

The Petersen graph is the most important graph in combinatorics. It has 10 vertices and 15 edges. It is the smallest bridgeless cubic graph with no 3-edge-colouring. It is a forbidden minor for linkless embedding. It appears in the proof of the four-colour theorem. It is a Moore graph, a Kneser graph, a strongly regular graph. It seems to show up everywhere.

The 27 lines on a cubic surface are the most classical objects in algebraic geometry. In 1849, Cayley and Salmon proved that every smooth cubic surface in three-dimensional projective space contains exactly 27 straight lines. The configuration of these lines—which ones meet and which ones are skew—is controlled by the exceptional Lie algebra E₆.

These two objects are connected. The connection is not a coincidence. It runs through the deepest structures in mathematics.

The blow-up

Every smooth cubic surface is the projective plane P² blown up at 6 points. You choose 6 points p₁,…,p₆ in general position and replace each point with a line. The result is a surface in P³, and it carries exactly 27 lines:

TypeCountDescription
eᵢ6Exceptional divisor over each blown-up point
gᵢⱼ15Proper transform of the line through pᵢ and pⱼ
fᵢ6Proper transform of the conic through all points except pᵢ

Two lines on the surface either meet in one point or are completely disjoint (skew). The meeting rules are combinatorial: the line gᵢⱼ meets gₖₗ if and only if {i,j} and {k,l} share an element. In other words, two “pair lines” are skew precisely when the pairs are disjoint.

The Petersen graph falls out

1 Take the 15 pair lines gᵢⱼ on the cubic surface. Two of them are skew when {i,j} ∩ {k,l} = ∅. This is the definition of the Kneser graph KG(6,2): vertices are 2-element subsets of {1,…,6}, edges connect disjoint pairs. The result is the triangular graph T(6), a strongly regular graph on 15 vertices.

2 Now forget one of the 6 blown-up points. Say we drop p₆. The 10 lines gᵢⱼ with i,j ∈ {1,…,5} still live on the surface. Their skewness pattern is the Kneser graph KG(5,2): 2-element subsets of a 5-element set, edges between disjoint pairs.

3 KG(5,2) is the Petersen graph.

The Petersen graph is the skewness graph of the 10 lines through pairs of 5 of the 6 blown-up points. Delete one point, get the Petersen graph.

A chain of three graphs

This gives us a chain of strongly regular graphs, each contained in the next:

GraphVerticesDegreeAutomorphism group
Petersen103S₅ (order 120)
T(6)156S₆ (order 720)
Schläfli2710W(E₆) (order 51840)

The automorphism groups grow: S₅, then S₆, then the full Weyl group of E₆. The jump from S₅ to S₆ is explained by the outer automorphism of S₆—the unique symmetric group that has one. The 15 pair partitions of a 5-element set are secretly the 15 edges of a complete graph on 6 vertices, and the hidden 6th vertex is what the outer automorphism reveals.

Why this is deep

The chain of graphs is a chain of Lie algebra inclusions:

sl(5) ⊂ sl(6) ⊂ E₆ | | | W(A₄) = S₅ W(A₅) = S₆ W(E₆) | | | Petersen T(6) Schläfli 10 = C(5,2) 15 = C(6,2) 27 = fund. rep

The 27 lines are the weights of the 27-dimensional fundamental representation of E₆. This representation knows about the entire configuration of the 27 lines: which ones meet, which are skew, and how they are permuted by symmetries. When we restrict this representation along the chain E₆ ⊃ A₅ ⊃ A₄, it decomposes:

E₆: 27 | restrict to A₅ = sl(6) A₅: 15 + 6 + 6 | restrict to A₄ = sl(5) A₄: 10 + 5 + 6 + 6

The 15 is the exterior square Λ²(C⁶)—the space spanned by 2-element subsets of a 6-element basis. This is the vertex set of T(6). The skewness relation (disjoint pairs) is the vanishing of a natural bilinear pairing on this exterior square.

The 10 is the exterior square Λ²(C⁵)—2-element subsets of a 5-element set. This is the Petersen graph. When T(6) restricts from sl(6) to sl(5), the 15 pair lines split as 10 + 5, and the 10-dimensional piece carries the Petersen graph as its skewness structure.

The Clebsch surface

There is one cubic surface where the whole chain becomes visible at once: the Clebsch diagonal surface, defined in P⁴ by

Σ xᵢ = 0, Σ xᵢ³ = 0

Its automorphism group is S₅, acting by permuting the five coordinates. This S₅ is the Weyl group W(A₄), and its action on the 27 lines decomposes them into two orbits:

OrbitSizeInduced graphAutomorphisms
Type I (pair partitions)15T(6) = SRG(15,6,1,3)S₆
Type II (five-cycles)125-regularC₂ × S₆

The 15 Type I lines form T(6), and inside T(6) sits the Petersen graph as the 10 lines that avoid the “hidden 6th point.” The chain of inclusions

Petersen ⊂ T(6) ⊂ Schläfli

is the geometric realization of

sl(5) ⊂ sl(6) ⊂ E₆

on a single cubic surface.

The spectral shadow

All three graphs share the eigenvalue 1. The corresponding eigenspaces form a nested chain:

dim 5 ⊂ dim 9 ⊂ dim 20 Petersen T(6) Schläfli

The 5-dimensional eigenspace of the Petersen graph is the standard representation of S₅. It sits inside the 9-dimensional eigenspace of T(6), which sits inside the 20-dimensional eigenspace of the Schläfli graph (an irreducible representation of PSp(4,3) ≅ Ω(5,3)).

Each eigenspace is an irreducible module for the corresponding group, and the inclusions are restrictions along the Lie algebra chain. The spectrum of a graph encodes the shadows of representation theory.

The Hecke algebras

The Petersen graph and the Schläfli graph are both Sabidussi coset graphs: the Petersen graph is Sab(A₅, S₃, HaH) and the Schläfli graph is Sab(PSp(4,3), (C₂⁴):A₅, HaH). In both cases, the pair (G, H) is a Gelfand pair—the Hecke algebra H(G,H) = EndG(C[G/H]) is commutative.

Both Hecke algebras have rank 3: three basis elements T₀, T₁, T₂ corresponding to the three double cosets (identity, adjacent, non-adjacent). Both are abstractly isomorphic to C³. But the structure constants—the multiplication rules—encode the SRG parameters:

Petersen: SRG(10, 3, 0, 1)

T₁² = 3T₀ + T₂ T₁T₂ = 2T₁ + 2T₂ T₂² = 6T₀ + 4T₁ + 3T₂

Schläfli: SRG(27, 10, 1, 5)

T₁² = 10T₀ + T₁ + 5T₂ T₁T₂ = 8T₁ + 5T₂ T₂² = 16T₀ + 8T₁ + 10T₂

The structure constant cijk counts two-step paths through the orbital graphs. For T₁² in the Petersen graph, the diagonal entry is 3 (each vertex has 3 neighbours), the adjacent entry is 0 (λ = 0, no common neighbours for adjacent vertices), and the non-adjacent entry is 1 (μ = 1, exactly one common neighbour for non-adjacent vertices).

The Petersen graph is not “a graph that happens to relate to the 27 lines.” It is the shadow of sl(5) inside E₆, made visible by the geometry of cubic surfaces. Every time you see the Petersen graph in mathematics—as a Moore graph, as a Kneser graph, as the obstruction in the four-colour theorem, as a forbidden minor for linkless embedding—you are seeing a fragment of E₆. The Clebsch surface is the cubic surface where this fragment is most clearly exposed, because its S₅ symmetry is exactly the Weyl group of the A₄ subalgebra sitting inside E₆.
